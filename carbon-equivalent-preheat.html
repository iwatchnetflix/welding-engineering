Carbon Equivalent in Preheat Assessment

Carbon-equivalent formulae reduce chemical composition to an empirical index associated with hardenability and hydrogen-cracking susceptibility. They are inputs to a preheat assessment; they are not preheat temperatures, weldability certificates or universal acceptance limits.

Common formulae

CEV = C + Mn/6 + (Cr+Mo+V)/5 + (Ni+Cu)/15
CET = C + (Mn+Mo)/10 + (Cr+Cu)/20 + Ni/40
Pcm = C + Si/30 + (Mn+Cu+Cr)/20 + Ni/60 + Mo/15 + V/10 + 5B

Element contents are entered as mass percentages. The formulae were developed from different steel populations and welding datasets. CEV, CET and Pcm are therefore not interchangeable, even when they produce similar numerical values. The formula required by the adopted assessment method must be used.

Engineering meaning

For comparable ferritic steels, a higher carbon-equivalent value generally indicates a greater potential for hard transformation products when the heat-affected zone cools rapidly. The value is useful for comparing heats, checking the composition represented by a qualified procedure and selecting the appropriate preheat-assessment method. It does not describe the thermal cycle or the completed joint.

Additional inputs to preheat assessment

InputReason it is required
Diffusible-hydrogen levelDepends on the welding process, consumable classification, conditioning and exposure
Combined thickness and joint geometryControl the heat sink and cooling rate around the weld
Heat input, preheat and interpass temperatureDetermine the temperature-time cycle with the joint geometry
Restraint and stress concentrationAffect the tensile stress acting while hydrogen redistributes
Steel product and delivery conditionQ&T, TMCP and normalized products can respond differently at the same nominal strength

Fixed CEV thresholds

A statement such as “CEV below 0.40 means that preheat is unnecessary” is not a complete engineering rule. A thin, low-restraint joint made with controlled low-hydrogen consumables may require no intentional preheat, while a thick or highly restrained joint from the same heat may require preheat. Tack welds, attachments and repairs can also present more severe local cooling or restraint than the principal production weld.

EN-based review sequence

  1. Identify the exact steel product, delivery condition, thickness and ladle analysis from the inspection document.
  2. Use the carbon-equivalent formula required by the governing method.
  3. Assign the hydrogen scale from qualified welding-consumable data and the actual handling procedure.
  4. Define combined thickness, joint geometry, heat input, restraint, initial temperature and environmental condition.
  5. Apply the project-adopted EN 1011-2 method and check any more restrictive product-manufacturer limits.
  6. Confirm that the resulting preheat and interpass requirements lie within the qualified WPS range and can be measured during production.
Carbon equivalent characterises composition. Preheat is determined from the interaction of composition, hydrogen, heat flow and restraint.
