Welding High-Strength Steel: Processes and Consumables

A beginner’s guide to welding steels in the nominal 690 MPa yield-strength class and above: understand the process, select compatible consumables, control the thermal cycle and verify the joint.

1. What are we joining, and why is it difficult?

Arc welding melts the joint edges and usually adds filler metal. The solidified weld metal joins the parts; the neighbouring parent metal is heated without melting and becomes the heat-affected zone (HAZ). The joint therefore contains three regions: weld metal, HAZ and unaffected parent metal.

The strength printed on the plate certificate is not automatically retained everywhere after welding. Rapid cooling can produce a hard, crack-sensitive HAZ; excessive thermal exposure can soften parts of a quenched-and-tempered HAZ or impair toughness. Consumable hydrogen and joint restraint also matter.

Examples include S690QL, S890QL and S960QL quenched-and-tempered plate, and Q690qE bridge steel. These are not interchangeable specifications. Confirm the product standard, full grade, delivery condition, actual thickness and inspection certificate; specified yield strength can vary with thickness.

The objective is a joint with the required strength, toughness and soundness—not simply a bead that looks neat.

2. Learn the words before choosing a process

TermPlain explanation
Consumable / filler metalWire, rod or covered electrode added to make the weld; SAW flux and shielding gas also form part of the consumable system.
Root, fill and cap passesThe first pass establishes the root; later passes fill the groove; the final passes form the visible surface. Different processes may be combined in one qualified procedure.
Deposition rateThe mass of weld metal deposited per unit time. High deposition rate does not by itself guarantee low total cost or acceptable properties.
Diffusible hydrogenHydrogen that can migrate in the weld metal. It is reported in mL per 100 g of deposited weld metal under a specified test method.
RestraintThe extent to which the joint and surrounding structure prevent weld shrinkage; strong restraint can increase cracking risk.
WPS / WPQR / PQRWPS gives production welding instructions. WPQR, or PQR in other code systems, records qualification conditions and test results. The governing code defines their scope.

3. Five common processes at a glance

Process / ISO 4063 numberHow the arc is protectedTypical useMain limitation
MAG with solid wire / GMAW — 135Externally supplied active gas mixtureFlexible shop fabrication and sheltered site work; root, fill or cap as qualifiedShielding, transfer mode and fusion must be controlled
MAG with flux-cored wire / FCAW-G — 136External active gas plus a flux-cored electrodeSuitable products offer good positional capability and productive fillingNeeds wind protection, slag removal and moisture control
SAW with solid wire — 121A granular flux blanket covers the arcMechanised long seams, typically flat butt or suitable horizontal fillet weldsFlux retention, access and the thermal window limit application
TIG / GTAW with solid filler — 141Inert gas protects a non-consumable tungsten electrode and poolControlled roots, thin material and selected precision workConventional manual TIG has a low deposition rate
MMA / SMAW — 111Electrode coating provides gas and slag protectionAccess-restricted joints, site work and qualified repairsElectrode changes, slag removal and hydrogen control affect productivity

GMAW is the broader gas metal arc welding name; MAG specifies an active shielding gas. Process 135 refers to solid wire, 136 to flux-cored wire with active gas, and 138 to metal-cored wire with active gas. A wire that contains a core is not automatically a 136 consumable. Process numbers identify the method, not a strength rating.

Reference: ISO 4063 — process nomenclature and reference numbers

4. Understand what each process needs

135 — Solid-wire MAG: a flexible starting point

A continuously fed wire is both electrode and filler metal. It is convenient for semiautomatic and robotic work, with no flux slag to remove. Positional welding depends on the transfer mode, parameter set, joint and qualified technique; one spray-transfer setting cannot be used in every position.

Pair a suitable high-strength solid wire with its specified shielding gas. An 80% Ar / 20% CO₂ mixture is one M21 example, not the definition of all M21 gases. Changing gas can change arc behaviour and deposited-metal properties. Pure CO₂ is not universally forbidden, but must be supported by the consumable classification, supplier data and WPS.

Keep the wire, groove and gas supply clean and dry. Provide an enclosure or effective wind shielding for outdoor work; adding gas flow indiscriminately does not reliably solve poor shielding.

Reference: TWI — MIG/MAG process and shielding

136 — Gas-shielded flux-cored welding: useful for positional work

Flux inside the wire influences arc behaviour, slag formation and weld-metal chemistry. Choose a product whose strength, toughness, hydrogen designation and permitted positions meet the application. Fast-freezing slag can help positional welding, but does not excuse poor fit-up, contamination or inadequate fusion.

Use the specified gas and remove slag between passes. FCAW-G still depends on external shielding gas and needs wind protection. Self-shielded FCAW-S is a different process, commonly numbered 114; assess its specific consumable and qualification evidence rather than assuming that every product has high hydrogen or is automatically suitable for S690–S960.

121 — Submerged arc welding: productivity within a thermal window

The arc burns beneath granular flux, allowing mechanised high-deposition welding. Butt welds are usually made flat; suitable fillet welds can also be made horizontal-vertical. The process is not a general-purpose vertical or overhead method because the molten pool and loose flux must be retained.

Select the wire and flux as a tested combination. Basic agglomerated fluxes are common candidates for demanding toughness and hydrogen requirements, but basicity alone is not evidence of compliance. Check the combination’s classification, condition of testing, hydrogen data and qualified heat-input range.

High deposition does not require excessive heat input. Wire size, current, speed, groove design and bead sequence must keep the joint within its qualified window. Recover and re-use flux only under a documented system that excludes contamination and controls particle condition.

Reference: TWI — SAW operating principle and positions

141 — TIG: control of the root and small weld pools

A non-consumable tungsten electrode produces the arc; a separate rod or wire supplies filler. Clean surfaces and inert shielding can give low hydrogen input. Conventional TIG is useful for controlled roots and thin sections, but is often slow for thick-joint filling. It is not restricted to root passes; mechanised and hot-wire variants have different productivity.

Use a compatible TIG filler and the specified inert gas, commonly argon. Do not substitute MAG active gas or add hydrogen to the shielding gas for these ferritic high-strength steels without an expressly supported procedure. Avoid tungsten contamination and verify the root geometry.

Reference: TWI — TIG applications and productivity

111 — Covered electrodes: access and handling discipline

The electrode melts while its coating provides protection. Suitable low-hydrogen basic electrodes are useful where access, portability or repair geometry favours manual welding. This is not inherently a repair-only process, but frequent stops and slag removal can reduce productivity.

Verify the electrode classification, polarity, positions and required drying or holding regime. Vacuum-packed products may have different initial handling instructions from opened packs. Track exposure time and returns; do not infer hydrogen performance from the word “basic” alone.

5. Select consumables by properties, not by a familiar code

Selection checkWhat to establish
Strength matchingCompare the relevant specified weld-metal and parent-metal properties under the governing design rules. Check yield strength and tensile strength separately. A grade number or a typical test result is not a guaranteed minimum.
Matching / undermatchingUndermatching can be beneficial in selected designs, but must be justified by joint capacity, geometry and required performance. It is not automatically acceptable simply because the joint is highly restrained.
Impact toughnessSpecify test temperature, absorbed energy, specimen size and test location. A −40 °C or −60 °C temperature alone is not an acceptance criterion; all-weld-metal data do not prove HAZ toughness.
HydrogenUse the limit required by the steel supplier and governing specification. A frequently used target is ≤5 mL/100 g, but it is not a universal legal limit for every 690 MPa steel. Read H4 and H5 under their respective classification standards.
Classification conditionsConfirm System A or B, shielding gas or wire–flux combination, as-welded or heat-treated condition, diameter and hydrogen suffix where applicable.
TraceabilityRetain the supplier data sheet, batch documentation and issue records. A consumable certificate and a WPQR demonstrate different things; neither replaces the other.

For S890–S960, verify that the proposed product and qualified joint can deliver the design properties. Do not buy a “G89” or “G96” family merely because its number resembles the steel grade: classification systems may use yield-based or tensile-based strength classes. This guide gives selection routes rather than unverified commercial recipes.

Reference: SSAB — diffusible hydrogen recommendation and handling

6. Control the thermal cycle and hydrogen together

VariableMeaning and practical control
Preheat temperatureTemperature reached before welding, measured in the required location. Establish it from composition, thickness, restraint, hydrogen and the governing method; higher-strength weld metal may also govern the minimum.
Interpass temperatureTemperature before depositing the next pass. Meet the required minimum and maximum; heat accumulation is not controlled by preheat alone.
Arc energy / heat inputEnergy per unit weld length. Heat-input calculations may include process efficiency; record the code convention, current, voltage and travel speed. Lower is not always better.
t8/5 cooling timeTime for the HAZ to cool from 800 °C to 500 °C. Appropriate limits depend on product and thickness; preheat, geometry and bead sequence also affect it.
Bead placement and sequenceControl bead size, interpass cleaning and distortion. Wide weaving or a different process sequence must remain within the supported WPS.
Hydrogen-release postheatingA controlled hold intended to aid hydrogen removal when required. It differs from stress-relief PWHT; neither is a universal mandatory step, and uncontrolled heating can alter quenched-and-tempered steel.

Keep electrodes and flux under the supplier’s drying, holding and exposure rules. Store wire to prevent condensation, rust and contamination. Flux-cored wire is not universally baked like a covered electrode: re-drying is product-specific and may be prohibited or ineffective. Quarantine suspect material and follow the documented recovery or rejection rules.

Reference: SSAB — cooling-time window and joint properties

Reference: Lincoln Electric — consumable storage and handling

7. Turn a proposed process into a qualified WPS

Start with the material, joint loading, access, position and fabrication setting. For a long shop butt seam, SAW may be efficient if its thermal cycle can be qualified. For sheltered site positional welds, 135 or an appropriate 136 product may be practical. A controlled TIG root followed by MAG fill is another candidate, but the combined sequence must be covered. These are selection examples, not approvals for a particular joint.

  1. Prepare a pWPS with the material range, process sequence, consumable/gas or flux, joint preparation, preheat, interpass limits and energy range.
  2. Weld and record a representative test coupon under the applicable qualification rules.
  3. Perform the prescribed inspection and tests: these can include visual/NDT, tensile, bend, impact, macro examination and hardness. The code and application determine which tests, locations, temperatures and limits apply.
  4. Review joint strength and fracture location, weld/HAZ toughness and hardness distribution; assess softening where relevant. A single maximum-hardness result cannot describe all joint properties.
  5. Use the accepted WPQR/PQR to establish the supported production WPS range. Verify personnel qualifications, equipment, consumable traceability and monitoring before production.

Selecting a certified wire does not qualify a procedure. Some codes permit other qualification routes or existing records; confirm applicability instead of assuming every consumable change requires a wholly new test, or that any existing PQR covers it.

Reference: ISO 15614-1 — procedure testing and qualification range
