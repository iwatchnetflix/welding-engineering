Yield-to-Tensile Strength Ratio in Structural Steel

The yield ratio compares the onset of yielding with the maximum engineering stress in a tensile test. It is useful for judging strength reserve beyond yield, but it is not a stand-alone measure of ductility, toughness, seismic energy dissipation or fracture safety.

Definition and terminology

Yield ratio: yield strength divided by tensile strength. The correct numerator depends on the material response and the governing product standard.

ReL is the lower yield strength, ReH is the upper yield strength, Rp0.2 is the 0.2% proof strength and Rm is the tensile strength. EN 10025-6 tabulates ReH for S690QL, while Rp0.2 is used when the tensile response does not show a distinct yield phenomenon. A reported ratio should therefore identify whether it is ReL/Rm, ReH/Rm or Rp0.2/Rm.

These properties are determined by a tensile test; ISO 6892-1 defines the room-temperature test method. A measured yield ratio should use the paired yield and tensile results from the same test result. It should not be assembled by dividing unrelated values from different heats, specimens or tables.

Design standards often express the related requirement in the inverse form, fu/fy. A maximum ReL/Rm and a minimum fu/fy describe the same direction of control, but measured test values and nominal design values are not automatically interchangeable.

What the ratio can indicate

Engineering questionWhat the yield ratio contributes
Strength reserve after first yieldA lower ratio means a larger stress interval between yield strength and tensile strength. It generally indicates more strain-hardening reserve before the maximum engineering stress is reached.
Plastic redistribution and capacity designThe separation between yield and tensile strength helps establish a hierarchy between yielding and fracture-controlled limit states. It is one input when design rules rely on plastic redistribution or component overstrength.
Comparison of production resultsFor the same product specification, thickness, delivery condition and test orientation, the ratio can help identify unusually small separation between measured yield and tensile strengths.

The ratio is a stress ratio, not a strain measurement. Saying that a lower value creates a larger “plastic deformation interval” is therefore imprecise unless elongation or the full stress–strain curve is also available.

What high and low values mean

In physical terms, yielding marks the start of permanent deformation. A high yield ratio means that, when permanent deformation begins, only a small increase in nominal stress remains before the tensile test reaches its maximum load. A lower ratio means that strain hardening can raise the stress by a larger amount after yielding before that maximum is reached.

Relative valueDirect interpretationWhat must not be concluded
High ratio, closer to 1.00Yield strength is close to tensile strength. The material has a smaller stress margin between first yield and maximum engineering stress, indicating less strain-hardening reserve in strength terms.It does not by itself prove low elongation, brittle behaviour or poor seismic performance.
Lower ratioYield strength is farther below tensile strength. The stress margin and indicated strain-hardening reserve are larger.It does not by itself prove adequate ductility, toughness or energy-dissipation capacity.
Simple example: suppose two tensile specimens both have Rm = 800 MPa. If specimen A yields at 760 MPa, its ratio is 760/800 = 0.95 and the stress interval from yield to Rm is only 40 MPa. If specimen B yields at 640 MPa, its ratio is 0.80 and the interval is 160 MPa. Specimen B therefore has the larger post-yield strength reserve before maximum load.

This is the physical meaning of the ratio: it describes the nominal stress reserve after yielding, not how close the steel is to fracture. Rm is the maximum engineering stress, not the fracture stress; deformation can continue after Rm as necking develops. Elongation, toughness and fracture behaviour must still be assessed separately.

Is there a preferred range?

There is no universal optimum range for all steels and structures. The acceptable value depends on the product standard, steel grade, thickness, structural design method, applicable National Annex and any project-specific capacity-design or procurement requirements.

Eurocode limits are acceptance boundaries, not target bands. Under EN 1993-1-1:2022, the recommended fu/fy limits correspond to fy/fu ceilings of about 0.91 for plastic global analysis and about 0.95 for elastic global analysis, unless the National Annex gives different values. These limits do not mean that 0.91 or 0.95 is an ideal value; they state the minimum separation required by the relevant design assumption.

How the parameter is used

  1. Define the ratio correctly. State whether the numerator is ReL, ReH, Rp0.2 or nominal fy, and use the corresponding Rm or nominal fu from the same basis.
  2. Apply the governing limit. Check the product standard, design standard, National Annex and project specification. Do not invent a general acceptance range.
  3. Keep nominal and measured values separate. Nominal fy/fu is used where the design rule requires product-standard values; an actual test ratio uses paired results from one tensile test.
  4. Check companion properties. Review elongation, toughness, fracture requirements, cyclic behaviour and connection detailing separately whenever they are relevant.

What the ratio cannot establish by itself

PropertyEvidence needed in addition to yield ratio
DuctilityElongation after fracture, uniform elongation or strain at maximum force, and—where relevant—the complete stress–strain response.
Resistance to brittle fractureApplicable Charpy impact requirements, fracture-toughness data where specified, service temperature, thickness, stress state and flaw assumptions.
Seismic or cyclic energy dissipationCyclic material and component behaviour, local-buckling resistance, connection detailing, weld and heat-affected-zone properties, loading history and system-level design rules.
Fatigue resistanceDetail category, weld geometry, imperfections, residual stress, stress range and inspection or execution quality.

A high yield ratio does not prove that a steel will fail suddenly after first yield. Conversely, a low ratio does not prove adequate toughness or cyclic ductility. The Hong Kong Code of Practice for the Structural Use of Steel treats strength, resistance to brittle fracture, ductility and weldability as separate essential material requirements; that separation is a useful engineering discipline.

S690QL worked example

S690QL is a quenched-and-tempered high-yield-strength structural steel for flat products under EN 10025-6:2019+A1:2022. For nominal thickness from 3 mm up to and including 50 mm, the product standard specifies a minimum yield strength ReH of 690 MPa, a tensile-strength range Rm of 770–940 MPa and a minimum elongation after fracture A of 14%. The L quality designates the specified Charpy impact properties at −40°C.

EN 10025-6 propertyS690QL, 3 mm ≤ t ≤ 50 mm
Delivery conditionQuenched and tempered
Minimum yield strength ReH690 MPa
Tensile strength Rm770–940 MPa
Minimum elongation after fracture A14%
Impact-quality designationQL: specified Charpy properties at −40°C

Nominal comparison is not a measured yield ratio

Using the specified minimum strengths for the thickness band above gives 690/770 = 0.896, or inversely 770/690 = 1.116. This is a comparison of nominal product-standard values and is relevant where a design rule calls for nominal fy and fu. It is not the measured ReH/Rm ratio of a delivered plate and it is not an acceptance ceiling of 0.896 for every tensile test.

An actual S690QL result must be calculated from the paired yield and tensile strengths reported for the same tensile test. The actual yield strength may exceed 690 MPa and the actual tensile strength may lie anywhere within the permitted range. For plate thicker than 50 mm, EN 10025-6 uses different strength bands, so the 690/770 comparison must not be reused.

Correct statement: for S690QL plate up to 50 mm, 0.896 is the ratio of the specified minimum ReH to the specified minimum Rm. The measured material yield ratio must come from paired certificate or test results.

Why a grade does not have one fixed measured ratio

S690QL identifies a product-standard grade, delivery condition and impact-quality level; it does not define one stress–strain curve for every heat and thickness. Statements such as “S690 has a yield ratio of 0.90” or “S355 has a yield ratio of 0.65” may describe selected nominal values or a particular test result, but neither is a universal measured property of the grade.

The distinction matters for high-strength steel. EN 1993-1-1 requires the applicable ductility checks to be satisfied, while the project design route and National Annex determine how S690 is used. Product-standard compliance, nominal design properties and actual certificate results should be recorded as three different things.

Strength utilisation and economy

A lower yield ratio does not mean that tensile strength is simply “wasted”. Many structural checks are governed by yield, stability, serviceability or fatigue rather than tensile rupture, so a large gap between yield and tensile strength may not translate directly into weight savings. Equally, the highest possible ratio is not an economic target because some design assumptions require reserve beyond yield.

The correct objective is compliance with the material product standard, structural design rules and project specification—not an isolated attempt to maximise or minimise the ratio.

Technical conclusion: use the yield ratio to quantify the separation between yield strength and tensile strength. A high value means a smaller strength reserve beyond yield; a lower value means a larger reserve. Acceptance must come from the governing standard and design method, and the ratio must not replace separate checks of elongation, toughness, fracture resistance or cyclic performance.
